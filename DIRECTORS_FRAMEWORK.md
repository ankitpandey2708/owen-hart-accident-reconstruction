# The Director's Framework: storytelling for any video

A video is world class when a stranger can say, after one viewing, what it was about, why it mattered, and what changed in them. This framework gets you there in eight stages, from the audience to the last frame. It applies to any length or genre: explainer, documentary, ad, launch film, training, short fiction.

It distils principles that working directors, screenwriters and editors use, paraphrased and adapted for short-form video:

| Principle | From |
|---|---|
| Controlling idea; every scene turns a value | Robert McKee |
| "Therefore / but", never "and then" | Trey Parker and Matt Stone |
| Suspense comes from what the audience knows that the character doesn't | Alfred Hitchcock |
| Cut for emotion first, then story, then rhythm | Walter Murch |
| Story spine; colour script | Pixar |
| What is set up must pay off | Anton Chekhov |
| Make the audience care about someone early | Blake Snyder |
| Opening and closing images that mirror each other | Common screenwriting practice |

**How to use it**

- **Work top-down.** Each stage depends on the one before it. A beautiful shot cannot rescue a missing controlling idea.
- **Scale, don't skip.** A 30-second video still has a hook, a turn and an ending; each one is just a single shot.
- **Write the answers down.** Every stage ends in a short written artefact (see [Templates](#templates)). If you can't write it in one or two sentences, the stage isn't done.
- **Run the [rubric](#review-rubric) before you build anything expensive**, and again on the first cut.

---

## Stage 1: Foundation

Decide four things before writing a single line: who the video is for, the one idea, the question that pulls the viewer through, and the logline.

**1. The audience contract.** Name one real viewer, not a demographic.

- Who are they, and what do they already know and believe?
- What do they feel at the start, and what should they feel at the end?
- What should they *do* or decide after watching?
- Where and how do they watch: a phone with no sound, a meeting-room screen, an autoplay feed?

**2. The controlling idea.** One sentence that states what changes and why: *[value] [shifts] because [cause]*. For example: "Trust collapses when one person can override the checks alone." Every scene must serve this sentence. A scene that doesn't is cut, however good it is.

**3. The dramatic question.** The question the viewer needs answered, planted in the first seconds and answered at the end. It can be *what happens*, *how* or *why*. A definition ("This is X") is not a question; a gap the viewer wants closed is.

**4. The logline.**
- Fiction: *when [a person] faces [a situation], they must [act] or [lose what's at stake]*.
- Non-fiction: *[subject] reveals [surprising truth] about [thing the viewer cares about]*.

**Gate:** a stranger reads the four answers and can tell you what the video is about and why they'd keep watching.

---

## Stage 2: Character and stakes

People watch people. Even a product explainer or a safety study needs a human the viewer cares about, because stakes are only felt through someone.

- **Want vs need.** The *want* is the visible goal (get the stunt done, ship the feature, win the case). The *need* is what they actually lack (a backup, the truth, help). The gap between the two is the story's engine.
- **Obstacle.** A force that makes the want hard: a person, a system, time, physics, their own flaw. No obstacle means no story, only a description.
- **Stakes, made concrete.** Answer "what is lost if this fails?" with something the viewer can picture: a person, a family, a job, a life. Abstract stakes ("efficiency", "risk") are not felt.
- **Early empathy.** Within the first 10–15% of the runtime, show the person doing something that makes us like or respect them, so their fate matters.
- **Non-fiction rule.** The film opens with the person or the situation the viewer recognises, and the device or data enters *inside* that situation as the answer. It returns to the person at the end. A film that opens on a diagram alone is a diagram, not a story.

**Gate:** you can say who we follow, what they want, what stands in the way, and what is lost if they fail.

---

## Stage 3: Structure

Structure is where the turns fall. Use these beats at any runtime and scale them by share of the total.

| Beat | Share of runtime | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook / opening image | 0–5% | The world and the person, plus the dramatic question. Frame 0 is the thumbnail: never black, never a logo. |
| Setup | 5–15% | What normal looks like, and what they want. Plant everything that pays off later. |
| Inciting incident | ~10–15% | The event that breaks normal and launches the story. |
| Rising action | 15–50% | Escalating attempts and complications, each caused by the last. |
| Midpoint turn | ~50% | A reversal or revelation that changes what the story is about. |
| Crisis | 70–80% | The worst moment, and the choice that defines the person. |
| Climax | 80–90% | The decisive event; the dramatic question is answered. |
| Resolution / closing image | 90–100% | The new normal, mirroring the opening image. |

**Scaling by length:**

| Runtime | Beats you keep | Shots per beat |
|---|---|---|
| 15–30 s (ad, social) | Hook → turn → payoff | 1 each |
| 60–90 s (explainer) | Hook, setup, inciting, rising, climax, closing | 1–3 |
| 3–5 min | All eight beats | 3–8 |
| 10–20 min (doc, training) | All eight, with sub-arcs inside rising action | Sequences of 2–3 min |

**Non-fiction variant (the investigation):** question → what we thought → what happened → why it was allowed to happen (the system, not only the part) → the cost → what must change → back to the person.

**Gate:** you can point to the timestamp of every turn, and each turn changes something.

---

## Stage 4: Scene design

A scene is the smallest unit that changes something. If nothing changes, it's a moment of information, and information belongs inside a scene that turns.

- **Every scene turns a value.** Note the scene's value charge at its start and end: safe → in danger, hope → despair, ignorance → knowledge. If start and end are the same, cut the scene or merge it.
- **Therefore / but.** Read the beats aloud joined by *therefore* or *but*. Wherever the joint is *and then*, causality is broken and the story reads as a list. Fix it until each beat is caused by the last.
- **Suspense through information control.** Decide for every scene who knows what: the viewer, the character, the narrator.
    - Viewer knows more than the character → suspense (we wait for it).
    - Viewer knows less → mystery (we chase it).
    - Both learn at once → surprise (one jolt, then it's gone).

    For known real events, the viewer already knows the outcome, so use suspense: show the danger long before the character meets it.
- **Setup and payoff.** Anything given weight early (an object, a line, a number) must return and matter. Anything that pays off must be set up. List both and connect them.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start each scene as close to its turn as possible, and leave the moment it lands.
- **One scene, one job.** If a scene has two jobs, split it or cut one.

**Gate:** every scene has a card (see [Templates](#templates)) with its value shift, its cause, and its payoff link.

---

## Stage 5: Visual storytelling

Show the story so that it works with the sound off; then add sound to make it felt.

- **One image per idea.** Each beat gets the single picture that carries its meaning. If it needs a caption to be understood, find a better picture.
- **Shot size is emotional distance.**
    - Wide: context, isolation, scale.
    - Medium: action and relationship.
    - Close-up: emotion and detail.
    - Insert or macro: the object that decides the story.

    Move closer as tension rises.
- **Point of view.** Decide whose eyes the camera borrows in each scene, and break that only with intent. A POV shift is a strong punctuation mark; use it at a turn.
- **Motif.** Choose one recurring visual element (an object, a colour, a shape, a framing) that changes meaning across the film. Its last appearance is the payoff.
- **Colour script.** Assign each act a palette and light quality that tracks the emotion (warm safety → cold danger → muted aftermath → clear resolution). Draw it as a strip of thumbnails before you shoot or render.
- **Composition carries meaning.** Height and space show power, negative space shows loneliness, and a frame within a frame shows a trap. Keep the screen direction consistent so the viewer never loses orientation.
- **Camera movement has a cause.** It moves because something in the story moves it: curiosity, threat, revelation. Movement for energy alone reads as filler.

**Gate:** a silent cut of the storyboard or animatic tells the story.

---

## Stage 6: Sound, rhythm and pacing

Sound is half of the feeling. Rhythm decides whether the feeling lands.

- **Silence is a tool.** The strongest moment is often the one where sound drops away. Plan one deliberate silence per film, at the crisis or the climax.
- **Music follows the emotion, not the picture.** Enter late, change at the turn, and hold back before the payoff. Never score every second.
- **Sound effects only on visible causes.** Every effect has a cause on screen, and too many read as a template.
- **Contrast creates rhythm.** Alternate long and short, loud and quiet, fast and slow. Uniform pacing is the most common reason a film feels flat.
- **Cut for emotion first.** When choosing a cut, weigh it in this order: does it serve the emotion, then the story, then the rhythm, then the viewer's eye line, then spatial continuity? A cut that is right emotionally beats one that is right technically.
- **Hold before the payoff.** A beat of stillness (0.3–1 s in short form, longer in long form) before the key moment makes it land.
- **Something new every few seconds** in short form (2–4 s), while the main idea holds still long enough to read.

**Gate:** a sound-only pass (eyes closed) still has shape, with tension, release and silence where you planned them.

---

## Stage 7: Words

Words carry what pictures can't, and nothing more.

- **Narration is sparse.** Short-form target: about 2.2 spoken words per second, and about 19 words per scene. Write it in phrases, so each reveal has a cue word.
- **Never say what the picture shows.** The voice adds the why, the consequence or the feeling. The screen never repeats the narration word for word.
- **Vary sentence length.** A long sentence builds; a short one lands. Use the short one after the long.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Use names, numbers, places and objects. "Seventy-eight feet" beats "a great height".
- **On-screen text is for the eye, not the ear.** Use labels on the thing they name, numbers that must be remembered, and one line per screen. Cap it at 6 words per line (10 for explainers), held 0.5 s plus 0.3 s per word.
- **Read it aloud, then read it to someone.** If they ask "what does that mean?", rewrite.
- **Banned:** jargon the audience doesn't use, hedging stacks ("may potentially"), slogans in place of meaning, and "Introducing…" or "Meet…" as an opening line.

**Gate:** the script read aloud runs to time, and a listener can repeat the controlling idea.

---

## Stage 8: Endings

The ending is what the viewer remembers, so design it first and write toward it.

- **Answer the dramatic question.** Every promise made in the hook is kept or deliberately broken.
- **Inevitable but surprising.** Looking back, the ending feels like the only one possible; looking forward, nobody saw it coming.
- **Mirror the opening image.** Return to the first picture, changed. The difference between the two images is the story.
- **Return to the person.** End on the human, not the mechanism or the logo.
- **Hand the viewer a question or an action.** A slogan is forgotten. A question they take back to their own life ("If this one part failed, what would hold?") stays with them.
- **Hold the last frame.** At least 2 seconds; the final line holds longer than any other.

**Gate:** someone who skipped to the last 10 seconds would still sense what the film was about.

---

## Truth and ethics

These apply to non-fiction and to any film about real people. Credibility is part of the craft: one wrong fact undoes the whole film.

- **Every claim traces to a source.** Keep a source sheet: claim, source link, date accessed, confidence.
- **Disputed is not silent.** Where sources conflict (a height, a cause, who decided), say that they conflict, and on screen if it matters.
- **Mark estimates.** Computed or illustrative numbers are labelled as such ("free-fall estimate").
- **Dignity for real people.** Reconstruct mechanics, not suffering. Use stand-ins (mannequins, silhouettes, diagrams) instead of likenesses, and avoid gore. Show what the audience needs to understand, not what would merely shock them.
- **No invented quotes, scenes or motives.** If you dramatise, label it.
- **Consider the people affected**: families, survivors, colleagues. Ask whether they would recognise the film as fair.

**Gate:** a fact-checker and someone close to the subject could both watch it without objection.

## The process

Move through these gates in order. Each one is cheaper than the next, so fix problems at the earliest gate possible.

1. **Brief**: the audience contract, controlling idea, dramatic question and logline (Stage 1).
2. **Research and source sheet**: facts, disputes and gaps (Truth and ethics).
3. **Three storylines**: three genuinely different films (different world, device and form). Pick the one a stranger would follow and remember.
4. **Beat sheet**: beats with timestamps and value shifts (Stages 3–4).
5. **Script**: narration and on-screen text, read aloud to time (Stage 7).
6. **Storyboard and colour script**: one frame per beat, plus the palette strip (Stage 5).
7. **Animatic**: rough pictures cut to scratch voice and temp sound. This is the cheapest place to fix pacing (Stage 6).
8. **Table read or test viewing**: show it to 2–3 people from the real audience. Ask them what it was about, the moment they felt most, and where they got bored.
9. **Production**: build the assets, render and record.
10. **Rough cut, then a rubric review** (below).
11. **Fine cut**: grade, mix and finish.
12. **Delivery**: master, subtitles, per-platform formats.

### Review rubric

Score each item from 1 (missing) to 5 (exemplary). Ship at 4+ on every item; anything at 3 or below is the next revision.

| # | Criterion | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Audience | Is it clearly made for one named viewer? |
| 2 | Controlling idea | Can a viewer state it in one sentence? |
| 3 | Hook | Does the first 5% plant a question they need answered? |
| 4 | Human stakes | Do we care about a person, and know what they could lose? |
| 5 | Causality | Does every beat join to the next with *therefore* or *but*? |
| 6 | Turns | Does every scene change a value? |
| 7 | Information control | Is suspense or mystery chosen on purpose? |
| 8 | Setup and payoff | Does everything planted pay off, and everything that pays off have a setup? |
| 9 | Visual storytelling | Does it work with the sound off? |
| 10 | Rhythm | Does it vary its pace, with a planned silence and a hold before the payoff? |
| 11 | Words | Is the narration sparse and concrete, never repeating the picture? |
| 12 | Ending | Does it answer the question, mirror the opening and hand the viewer something to carry out? |
| 13 | Truth | Is every claim sourced, disputes stated and estimates marked? |
| 14 | Dignity | Would the people affected recognise it as fair? |

## Templates

### One-page story brief

```markdown
# <Working title>

**Audience:** <one named viewer: who, what they know, where they watch>
**They should feel:** <start feeling> → <end feeling>
**They should do:** <the action or decision after watching>
**Controlling idea:** <value> <shifts> because <cause>
**Dramatic question:** <the question planted in the hook and answered at the end>
**Logline:** <one sentence>
**Person we follow:** <who> · wants <want> · needs <need> · blocked by <obstacle>
**Stakes:** <what is lost, concretely>
**Runtime and format:** <length, aspect, platform, sound on or off>
**Opening image → closing image:** <first picture> → <last picture, changed>
**Motif:** <recurring element and how its meaning changes>
**Open questions / disputed facts:** <list>
```

### Beat sheet

```markdown
| # | Beat | Time | Value at start → end | Caused by (therefore/but) | Picture | Sound | Words |
|---|------|------|----------------------|---------------------------|---------|-------|-------|
| 1 | Hook | 0:00–0:04 | calm → unease | — | <one image> | <bed, silence, effect> | <line> |
| 2 | Setup | … | … | therefore … | … | … | … |
```

### Scene card

```markdown
**Scene <n>: <name>**  (<start>–<end>, <seconds> s)
- Job: <the one thing this scene does>
- Value: <start> → <end>
- Cause: <therefore / but, linked to scene n-1>
- Who knows what: viewer <…> / character <…> → suspense | mystery | surprise
- Picture: <shot size, POV, the one image>
- Sound: <music change, effect with its on-screen cause, or silence>
- Words: <narration line ≤ 19 words> / <on-screen text ≤ 6 words>
- Plants: <setup this scene plants>  ·  Pays off: <earlier setup it pays off>
```

### Source sheet (non-fiction)

```markdown
| Claim | Source (link) | Accessed | Confidence | Disputed? | On screen as |
|-------|---------------|----------|------------|-----------|--------------|
| <claim> | <url> | <YYYY-MM-DD> | high / medium / low | <yes: versions> | <fact / "reported" / estimate> |
```
